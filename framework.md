# The Battlecard Framework

A good battlecard answers one question fast: **"The prospect just mentioned [Competitor X], what do I say in the next 30 seconds?"**

Everything below is built around that test. If a section doesn't help a rep in that moment, cut it.

---

## 1. Snapshot

Three lines, no more:
- **Who they are**: one sentence, no jargon.
- **Why they win**: the scenario where they're genuinely the better choice. Say it honestly; sales won't trust the rest of the card if this section is spin.
- **Why they lose**: the scenario where you win, stated as a fact rather than an insult.

## 2. Company & funding context

Only include this if you have valuable information that can influence the conversation: recent funding round (aggressive discounting likely?), leadership departure, layoffs, being acquired. Skip it if it's just background noise.

## 3. Positioning comparison

The competitor's tagline/category claim vs. yours, side by side.  
This exposes what's actually at stake: is it a category disagreement, a segment disagreement, or a feature fight?
**Category disagreement**: you and the competitor aren't even claiming to solve the same problem. The rep's job isn't to compare features; it's to re-anchor the conversation on what the prospect is actually trying to solve, and let the category claim do the work.
**Segment disagreement**: you agree on the category, but you're built for different buyers (company size, technical maturity, use case complexity). The rep's job is to qualify fast: does this prospect actually look like the competitor's ideal customer, or yours?
**Straight feature fight**: same category, same segment, and the deal genuinely comes down to capabilities. This is the only case where a feature-by-feature comparison (section 5) is the right tool — using it in the other two cases is exactly the "feature parity list" trap the framework is built to avoid.

## 4. Pricing & packaging

- Share the entry price, and what's actually included at that tier (not just the official listed price, as the traps are usually in the tier limits).
- Where their pricing model creates friction as the customer grows: per-seat pricing that scales up as the team grows even if usage hasn't changed, usage limits that require an upgrade once you outgrow them, or annual-only contracts that reduce flexibility.
- Where your model is a true advantage and where it's a disadvantage. You need to state both.

## 5. Capability comparison

Don't build a full feature-comparison table: nobody reads those, and they're too hard to maintain.  
Pick the 4-6 capabilities that actually come up in deals and are genuinely differentiated. For each: what they do, what you do, and the one-sentence "so what" for the buyer.

## 6. Where they win / where you win

Segment it by use case, buyer persona, or company size, not by feature. This is the section reps actually use to qualify early: "if the prospect looks like Y, expect Competitor X."  
You can also add a few customer references, or recent wins and losses.

## 7. Objection handling

The actual phrases prospects use when they bring up this competitor, each with a short, honest response, not something that sounds too scripted, a real answer a rep can say out loud without sounding defensive.

> "[Competitor X] is cheaper." → [response]
> "[Competitor X] has [feature] and you don't." → [response]
> "We're already using [Competitor X] for Y." → [response]

## 8. Trap-setting questions

Discovery questions that surface the competitor's known weak points naturally, without ever naming them.  
These should sound like normal discovery, not a trap, because it isn't one. It's just asking about something the prospect hasn't thought to check yet.

## 9. Proof points

Customer stories, review-site quotes, benchmark data, anything a rep can point to as evidence rather than opinion.  
If you don't have real proof points for a given claim, mark it as a gap rather than inventing one, an unproven claim used on a call can hurt you more than help you.

---

## What good looks like

- A rep can read the whole thing in under 3 minutes.
- Every claim is either sourced or clearly marked as "unverified".
- It gets updated when the competitor releases a major feature, not on a fixed quarterly schedule that's already outdated by the time it's shared.
- Sales actually opens and reads the battlecard before a call, which only happens if they trust it.
